# BLUFF-SCENARIOS.md — how bluff scoring and the points total actually play out

This walks through three players — a novice, a mediocre player, and a pro — over 30 days, as the game itself grows from a handful of friends to a large crowd. Every number below is **generated by `node Tools/bluff-scenarios.mjs`**, which runs the real engine code (`js/engine.js`, `js/rating.js`, `js/config.js`) — nothing here is hand-calculated, so it reflects exactly what the app will do. Re-run that script and paste its output over the tables after any change to `SCORING`, `STREAK_BONUS`, or `POINTS`.

## The two mechanisms that produce this shape

**1. Bluff points are `12 × √(people fooled)`, per word.** Fooling 1 person always earns 12. Fooling 25 earns 60. Fooling 400 earns 240. Each additional person fooled is worth a little less than the last, but there is no ceiling — a bluff that fools a real crowd keeps earning more, indefinitely.

**2. Your points total is a running sum: `total = total + today's points`.** There is one points number in this game, and a day's result moves it by exactly what the score screen said. That's a deliberate replacement for the old lifetime-average rating (`800 + sum/days`), under which a screen saying "+390" could move the header by +23 — or *down*, if 390 happened to be below your average. See `js/rating.js`'s header comment.

The direct consequence, visible in every table below: **totals only ever go up (until a rare 0/3 day), and they grow roughly linearly with how well and how often you play.** There's no flattening-out and no ceiling; a good day is worth the same whether it's your 2nd or your 200th.

## What the average bought, and what replacing it cost

The old average had one real virtue worth naming, since it's gone: a skilled newcomer could overtake a veteran immediately, because the average only ever averaged *your own* days. A running total can't do that — see the last table. The tradeoff was made deliberately: an unreadable headline number that contradicts the score screen every single day is a worse problem than a leaderboard that partly rewards seniority, especially in a daily-ritual game where showing up *is* the game. The streak bonus (up to +70%) is what keeps consistency meaningful without needing the average to do it.

## Why not the earlier bluff formulas

- **Flat rate per vote (the original idea):** a bluff earned a fixed number of points per person fooled, with no ceiling. The problem: as the *player base* grows, a popular word's total payout scales linearly and unboundedly, which can dwarf every other number in the game.
- **A fixed pool split by percentage (the first revision):** capped a word's total payout at a fixed pool, split by *share* of that word's guessers. This fixed the unbounded-scaling problem but broke down at small scale: fooling 1 out of 1 total guesser is a 100% share, which claimed the *entire* pool for what could easily be a coin flip between two friends.
- **`12 × √(fooled count)` (current):** depends only on the *absolute* number of people fooled, never on what fraction of some total that is. Fooling 1 of 1 and fooling 1 of 300 both earn the same 12 points — fooling one person is fooling one person, regardless of how many others didn't see it. But fooling 100 people, out of however many, earns far more than fooling 1 — because that's a bigger, rarer feat, full stop.

## The three players

Illustrative assumptions used below (not measured — see `js/config.js` header for the "retune freely" rule):
- **Novice:** gets 1 of 3 guesses right most days (the guessing score's breakeven point). Writes weak bluffs that reliably fool about 1 person, regardless of how big the game gets — a bluff that isn't very convincing doesn't suddenly convince more people just because more people are looking at it.
- **Mediocre:** gets 2 of 3 right most days. Writes bluffs that fool roughly 15% of that word's guessers.
- **Pro:** gets 3 of 3 right most days. Writes genuinely convincing bluffs that fool roughly 35% of that word's guessers.

The game itself grows in three phases: a small friend group (3 guessers/word, days 1–10), a growing community (30 guessers/word, days 11–20), and a large, popular game (300 guessers/word, days 21–30). **Today the real game is firmly in the first phase** (~10 players), which is the column to read if you want to know what a number on screen will look like this week.

The pro shows the same shape at a higher level throughout, and benefits the *most* from the game growing, since a genuinely convincing bluff fools a much bigger absolute number of people once the audience is large (105/word once the game is popular, versus 1/word for the novice in the exact same crowd).

**One thing to watch as the game grows:** at 300 guessers/word, bluff points (369/day) dwarf guess points (75/day), and daily numbers reach the high hundreds. That's the deliberate, documented consequence of the no-ceiling `√` curve — but it means the "a day is worth double or triple digits" sizing that holds at today's scale would need `bluffBaseK` retuned (or a soft cap introduced) if the game ever actually reached that size. At the current ~10 players, none of this bites: a pro earns 122–189/day and takes most of a year to reach five digits.

### A new, equally-skilled player joins on day 21

Same skill as the Pro above (3/3 guesses, fools 35% of guessers), but with zero history, starting once the game is already large:

| 30 | +755 | **6,616** | **12,525** |

Under the old average-based rating, the newcomer overtook the veteran on their very first day. Under a running total they don't — they close the gap steadily (the veteran's early days were earned in a much smaller game, so the newcomer out-earns them per day and would eventually pass them), but seniority is now worth something. This is the cost named at the top of this file, stated plainly rather than hidden: it's the price of the score screen and the header finally agreeing.
